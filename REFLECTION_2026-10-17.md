# Where people are thinking wrong about their needs (2026-10-17)

Built from `demand.duckdb` (72,881 US job postings, 30 sectors) and `pessimism.duckdb`
(500 scored cases, both verdicts). Every number is a query; the base rates are biased by
selection (see the pessimists-archive skill, rule 1), so read them as relative, not absolute.

## The vein: the people surest they don't need AI are the ones whose postings are mostly paperwork

| Sector | Postings mentioning AI | …mentioning scheduling | …paperwork / documentation / charting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restaurants & hospitality | 0.3% | 59% | 6% |
| Senior & home care | 0.4% | 64% | 25% |
| Social services | 0.9% | 61% | 52% |
| Construction | 1.2% | 69% | 34% |
| Healthcare | 3.1% | 64% | 44% |
| Retail | 0.9% | 72% | 6% |
| Software & IT | 26.7% | 29% | 42% |

The Archive's pattern for this: "it won't work / it's not for us" claims were wrong 68% of the
time, and in 59 cases the group that resisted became the user. Merryweather, who built horse-drawn
fire engines, called the car "an amusing toy" in 1896, then sold motor fire engines. In 1952 UNIVAC
called the election within four electoral votes; the operators "decided UNIVAC must have made a
mistake," and the forecast never aired.

What they will need is not "AI". It is the step: the chart note, the schedule, the form. Asked,
they will say they need staff. That's where to look:

1. **Back office for hands-on operators.** The duty lines tagged as doable by a tool, most common first:
   filing documents, tracking inventory, building reports, entering data, keeping CRM records up to date,
   booking service appointments, comparing vendor quotes.
2. **Coverage, not headcount.** 63% of retail postings and 62% of senior-care postings are part-time.
   They post jobs to buy hours, so the real need is filling shifts.
3. **Training they already pay for.** 45% of security and policing postings, 30% of automotive and 28%
   of senior care offer paid training. The buyer exists; they just don't call it software.
4. **Credential paperwork.** CDL in 38% of transportation postings, a pharmacy licence in 32% of pharma,
   a real-estate licence in 27%, a state guard card in 25% of security. Region changes the paperwork,
   not the job.
5. **The professions loudest against it.** In 1936 bar associations had a free legal-advice radio show
   pulled as "contrary to public interest"; New York is now moving against AI legal aid. Paralegals
   outnumber attorneys about 2 to 1 in legal postings, and 7% of legal postings already mention AI.

An analog only says the objection is weak. Whether they use it is learned by shipping
(BUSINESS.md rule 2). The outreach ledger agrees on how to get it in front of them: opening on
their own record produced 4 of the 5 positive replies on file. They don't believe the category
until it is their own paperwork, done.

## Where today's fear is probably right

- **Money.** Financial warnings came true 48% of the time and investors' warnings 70%. The AI capex
  arithmetic sits in the category that comes true. The bicycle had a bubble that burst in 1897
  ("when do you think this bicycle bubble will collapse", 1894), and the bicycle stayed. Both can
  be true: the spending can crash while the technology becomes universal.
- **Privacy.** 33% right and 60% mixed. Camera glasses and agents with inbox access belong here.
- **Specific jobs.** Job fears were 53% mixed: wrong in aggregate, right for named occupations. In 1953
  "the union fears many of its members may be thrown out of work" about automatic elevators, and it
  was right. The front desk, data entry and the call center are today's version. Their postings still
  look healthy, but US horse numbers kept rising until about 1915, after the car arrived. Today's
  hiring is not tomorrow's need.

## Where today's fear is probably wrong

Youth and morals fears: 0 of 80 came true. Video-game fears were 93% wrong, dating-app fears 93%,
AI-companion fears 78%. Don't build a "protect them from X" business on a moral panic.

## The test to run on any idea

Name the objection and who is raising it. A user saying "won't work, not for us" is a weak
objection. An insider or investor pointing at something specific, mechanical and near-term (a
balance sheet, an O-ring) is a strong one. Fears that came true were settled in a median of
4 years; fears that proved wrong took 20.
